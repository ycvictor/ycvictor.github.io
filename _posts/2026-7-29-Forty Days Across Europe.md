# Forty Days Across Europe

Solo travel and visiting Europe had always been on my bucket list. After finally landing a job, I decided it was the perfect time to spend forty days traveling across Europe.

Unlike my previous trips, where I planned every detail and packed each day with activities, this time I wanted to slow down and simply go with the flow.

Before leaving, I wasn't particularly worried about much. The only thing I kept hearing was how common theft could be, and since every budget airline seemed to have different baggage rules, I decided to travel with just a backpack that was small enough to fit in hostel lockers. Every day, I went out carrying only my phone and a portable charger.

What surprised me the most was the excitement I felt before the trip. It reminded me of how I used to feel as a kid before going abroad. I hadn't felt that kind of anticipation in a long time.

The journey started in Paris, then continued through Prague, Vienna, Budapest, Kraków, Warsaw, Nuremberg, Munich, Barcelona, and finally back to Paris.

Looking back, I think my mindset changed in three distinct stages.

At the beginning, everything felt new. I didn't want to miss a single landmark or the story behind every place I visited. I was amazed by how beautiful every building was, how magnificent the churches and palaces were, and I wanted to try every bakery that people recommended.

By the time I reached Vienna, I found myself getting tired of walking through yet another church or palace. Instead, I became much more interested in the history behind them. Whenever I came across a statue, I would stop and look it up, wanting to understand who that person was and why they deserved to be remembered.

That curiosity eventually led me to learn more about World War II. As I traveled farther east, I visited Auschwitz, where one of the largest Nazi concentration camps was located, and later Nuremberg, a city that played a significant role in the history of Nazi Germany. Visiting these places gave me a much deeper understanding of the stories behind the monuments and the lasting impact history still has on Europe today.

Later, when I arrived in Barcelona, my perspective had shifted again. If I didn't have enough time to see a particular attraction, I was completely fine skipping it. After all, I could always come back one day. Instead, I focused on experiencing the city itself and appreciating the people I met along the way. Those moments turned out to be far more memorable than many of the famous landmarks.

When the trip finally came to an end, I felt like I had collected another piece of myself. It was another reminder that I was capable of doing something I had always wanted to do. And somehow, the world felt just a little bit bigger than it had before.
